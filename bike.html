Reaction-wheel self-balancing bicycle

Spring 2026 · ME 2011, University of Minnesota · independent project · $39.54 in parts

In 60 seconds

  • Built: SolidWorks model → 3D-printed frame parts on 608z bearings and a laser-cut reaction wheel → Arduino UNO + MPU-6050 IMU → Nidec 24H brushless motor driving the wheel → PID loop written in C++.

  • Hardest bug: the motor would not respond to its driver. An isolated test program showed the supplier had swapped the PWM, direction and start signals against their own documentation.
  • Then: reinforced the frame, added servo steering, non-blocking stepper propulsion, a servo-actuated kickstand that retracts for hands-free launches, and voltage regulation.

Why a reaction wheel

A stationary bicycle is an inverted pendulum: once it tips, gravity pulls it further. A reaction wheel fixes that without any forward motion. Spinning the wheel up in one direction pushes a reaction torque back on the frame in the other, so the controller can "catch" a tilt the instant the IMU sees it. The trade-off is that a wheel can only produce torque while it is accelerating, so the controller has to keep the wheel near its resting speed and act early rather than hard.

Design

Everything was chosen to be cheap and makeable with the tools I had: printed structure running on 608z bearings, a laser-cut disc for the reaction wheel (no machining needed), an Arduino UNO for control, an MPU-6050 for tilt angle and rate, and a high-speed Nidec 24H brushless motor. The whole build came to $39.54.

Control

The IMU gives tilt angle and angular rate; a PID loop on the Arduino turns that into a motor command for the reaction wheel. The wheel's angular acceleration produces the counter-torque that pushes the frame back toward upright.

What went wrong, and what I did about it

The brushless motor would not respond to its driver. Rather than keep changing the balance code, I wrote an isolated motor test program that exercised each control line on its own, and found the supplier had swapped the PWM, direction and start signal assignments against their own documentation. Rewiring to the true pin-out restored control. After the first balancing runs I reinforced the frame, then added an MG996R servo to steer the front wheel, a 28BYJ-48 stepper for rear-wheel propulsion written non-blocking so the balance loop is never starved, a servo-actuated kickstand that retracts automatically for hands-free launches, and voltage regulation.
